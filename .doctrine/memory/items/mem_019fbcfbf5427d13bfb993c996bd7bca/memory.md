Writing "this may be a false positive" beside an inference does not license the
inference. It documents that you knew. A defect you disclose is still a defect,
and a reviewer is entitled to raise it — the disclosure only removes the charge
of concealment.

**Why.** RV-325 F-16 (SL-233): a pre-registered falsifier inferred "candidate
boundary text could not be produced, therefore no such boundary exists", and the
paragraph five lines below conceded that non-production could equally be failure
to invent. Both were on the page together. The disclosure had been offered for
three consecutive rounds as the closing move of each response; the fourth round
raised it as a finding, correctly.

**How to apply.**
1. When you catch yourself writing a residual-disclosure paragraph, ask whether
   it names an *invalid step* or an *accepted cost*. An invalid step is a defect
   — repair it or route the result to `inconclusive`. Only accepted costs
   legitimately live in a disclosure.
2. The usual repair for "failure of search" reasoning is to **bound the domain**:
   find the finite, enumerable thing the unbounded search was standing in for.
   (Here: enumerate candidate *moments* in the run record, not candidate
   *wordings*.) An exhaustive check over an enumerable domain licenses a
   conclusion; a failed search over an unbounded one licenses nothing.
3. Scope the conclusion to what the bounded check shows, and make the resulting
   action **defeasible** on the artefact that would have gone the other way. That
   keeps the expensive outcome reachable without claiming a proof you don't have.
4. Check the remedy against earlier findings: reversing a prior repair to satisfy
   a new one restores a defect the review already removed.
